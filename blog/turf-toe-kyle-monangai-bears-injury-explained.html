The Bears' Running Back and Turf Toe: Why a 'Toe Sprain' Can Sideline Players for Weeks

November 4, 2026 · 5 min read

Chicago Bears running back Kyle Monangai has been sidelined by a turf toe injury for much of October. ESPN's Courtney Cronin, reporting for TSN on October 21, wrote that Monangai had been ruled out for a third straight game. Coach Ben Johnson had called the toe injury a "bigger deal" than the team first expected, and it led to a visit with a specialist. The team listed him as week-to-week rather than placing him on injured reserve.1 Heading into Week 9, FantasyPros described the injury as severe turf toe, with Monangai out for multiple games.2

We can't speak to any individual's injury beyond what has been publicly reported. But "turf toe" is a common football injury that is often underestimated, so here is what it actually involves.

What Is Turf Toe?

Turf toe is a sprain of the joint at the base of the big toe, the first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint. It typically happens when the big toe is forcibly bent upward (hyperextended), for example when a player's foot is planted and weight drives forward over the toes. The injury affects the plantar plate complex: the ligaments, joint capsule, and small sesamoid bones on the underside of the joint that stabilize it during push-off.3,4

Who Gets It?

A study of five seasons of NCAA injury surveillance data found that football players were nearly 14 times more likely to sustain turf toe in games than in practice. Running backs and quarterbacks were the most commonly affected positions. Injury rates were also significantly higher on third-generation artificial turf than on natural grass. Fewer than 2% of injuries in that study required surgery.5

Why Severity Matters So Much

Turf toe injuries are graded by how much of the plantar plate complex is damaged. A 2023 review summarized typical time lost by grade:3

  • Grade I (stretch or minor injury): return often within about 3 to 5 days once weight-bearing is comfortable.
  • Grade II (partial tear): typically 2 to 4 weeks, sometimes with taping on return.
  • Grade III (complete disruption of the plantar plate): 4 to 6 weeks or more, depending on treatment.

The same review noted that athletes with low-grade injuries usually return to their prior level of performance. Among athletes with higher-grade injuries, about 70% are expected to maintain their level of performance.3

How It's Treated

Most turf toe injuries are treated without surgery. Treatment commonly includes rest, ice, compression, protection or immobilization when needed, and a structured rehabilitation program. Surgery is generally reserved for severe injuries in which the plantar restraints are torn and the joint becomes unstable.6 MRI is often used to classify the extent of the injury, because the specific structures involved help guide treatment.4

The Bottom Line

"Turf toe" sounds minor, but it covers a range of injuries to the big toe joint's stabilizing structures. The more severe forms can sideline athletes for weeks.3,5 Because the big toe is critical for push-off, getting the grade right and fully restoring the joint's function matter for a safe return.
