The Bears' Quarterback and a Hamstring Injury: Understanding Hamstring Strains

September 28, 2026 · 6 min read

Chicago Bears fans held their breath on September 20 when quarterback Caleb Williams was carted off late in the fourth quarter of a 9-3 loss to the Minnesota Vikings. The Associated Press reported that he was hurt on a scramble and left with a right hamstring injury.1 The next day, the Chicago Sun-Times reported that an MRI showed no major damage. Coach Ben Johnson described the injury as likely "a week-to-week deal."2

We can't speak to the specifics of any individual's injury beyond what has been publicly reported. But hamstring strains are among the most common muscle injuries in sport, so here is what the research says about them in general.

What Is a Hamstring Strain?

The hamstrings are three muscles along the back of the thigh that bend the knee and extend the hip. A strain is an injury to the muscle fibers or the muscle-tendon junction. Injuries range from mild, with minimal structural damage, to more extensive tears. Athletes often describe a sudden pain or "pop" in the back of the thigh during sprinting or reaching.

Hamstring injuries generally come in two patterns. Sprinting-type injuries happen during high-speed running. Stretching-type injuries happen when the muscle is lengthened to an extreme, as in high kicks or splits. In one randomized trial, stretching-type injuries took noticeably longer to return from than sprinting-type injuries.3

How Common Are They?

In a 13-year study of European professional soccer clubs, about 22% of players sustained at least one hamstring injury per season. Hamstring injury rates rose over the study period, particularly injuries in training.4

The Biggest Risk Factor: A Previous Hamstring Injury

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled data on more than 8,000 hamstring strains. A history of hamstring injury was among the strongest risk factors, roughly tripling the risk, and a recent hamstring injury raised it nearly fivefold. Older age, previous ACL injury, and previous calf strain were also associated with higher risk.5 That is why the quality of rehabilitation after a first injury gets so much attention.

What Rehab Research Shows

A randomized trial in elite Swedish soccer players compared two rehabilitation programs after acute hamstring injury. One emphasized "lengthening" exercises, which load the hamstring while it is in a stretched position. The other used conventional exercises. Players in the lengthening program returned to full training in an average of 28 days, compared with 51 days for conventional exercises.3

What Prevention Research Shows

The Nordic hamstring exercise is a partner-assisted eccentric exercise in which an athlete slowly lowers the body forward from a kneeling position. It has some of the strongest prevention evidence in sports medicine. A meta-analysis of more than 8,400 athletes found that programs including the Nordic hamstring exercise reduced hamstring injury rates by about half.6

The Bottom Line

Hamstring strains are common, can range widely in severity, and have a strong tendency to recur.4,5 Rehab that progressively loads the hamstring in lengthened positions, and prevention programs that include eccentric strengthening, have meaningful evidence behind them.3,6
