An Achilles Tear in the NBA Preseason: How Achilles Ruptures Happen and How They're Treated

October 7, 2026 · 6 min read

The NBA preseason opened with an injury no player wants to hear about. In the Philadelphia 76ers' preseason opener against the Knicks on October 5, center Ariel Hukporti went down in the first quarter on a non-contact play while defending. NBC Sports Philadelphia reported that an MRI confirmed a torn right Achilles tendon. The team said he underwent successful surgery and is out indefinitely.1

We can't comment on any individual's injury beyond public reports. But Achilles ruptures are a well-studied injury, and the research on how they are treated has changed meaningfully over the last 15 years.

What Is an Achilles Rupture?

The Achilles tendon connects the calf muscles to the heel bone. It is the largest tendon in the body and transmits enormous force during running and jumping. A rupture is a complete or near-complete tear, often during a sudden push-off or quick change of direction. People commonly describe feeling as if they were kicked or hit in the back of the heel, sometimes with an audible pop, followed by weakness pushing off the foot.

Surgery vs. Non-Surgical Treatment

For decades, surgical repair was widely viewed as the default, especially for athletes. Non-surgical treatment traditionally meant long periods in a cast, and that approach was associated with more re-ruptures. The picture changed as non-surgical care shifted to accelerated functional rehabilitation, with early weight-bearing and early controlled motion in a boot.

A multicenter randomized trial published in 2010 compared surgery with non-surgical treatment, with both groups using accelerated rehabilitation. Re-rupture rates were low and similar: two patients in the surgical group and three in the non-surgical group. Strength, range of motion, and function were clinically similar between groups. Complications, mainly related to soft tissue and wound healing, were more common after surgery.2

A 2019 meta-analysis in the BMJ pooled 29 studies. Overall, surgery reduced re-rupture risk (2.3% versus 3.9%), but it carried a higher rate of other complications (4.9% versus 1.6%), mostly infection. In studies that used accelerated functional rehabilitation, the difference in re-rupture rates was no longer statistically significant. The authors emphasized shared decision-making based on patient-specific factors.3

A large Norwegian randomized trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2022 compared non-surgical treatment, open repair, and minimally invasive repair in 554 adults. At 12 months, patient-reported outcomes and physical performance were similar across all three groups. Re-ruptures were more common with non-surgical treatment (6.2% versus 0.6% in each surgical group). Nerve injuries were more common with minimally invasive surgery.4

What About Elite Athletes?

The trials above mostly enrolled recreational adults rather than professional athletes, so how well their findings apply to elite jumping and sprinting athletes is less certain. In elite sport, treatment decisions also weigh factors like position demands and timing within a career. Whatever the treatment, recovery from an Achilles rupture is generally measured in many months.

The Bottom Line

Both surgical and non-surgical treatment can produce good outcomes for an acute Achilles rupture when paired with modern functional rehabilitation.2–4 Surgery modestly lowers re-rupture risk at the cost of a higher rate of other complications.3,4 That trade-off is why the decision is individualized.
