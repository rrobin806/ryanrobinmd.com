Hockey Season and Groin Strains: What the Evidence Says About Adductor Strength

October 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Hockey season is underway at every level, from youth programs in suburban rinks to the NHL. Skating places heavy, repetitive demands on the inner-thigh (adductor) muscles, and groin strains are a well-known problem in the sport. They are also common in soccer, where the same muscles work hard during kicking and cutting. Here is what the research says about why these injuries happen and what can lower the risk.

Not All Groin Pain Is the Same

"Groin pain" covers several different problems. To bring order to the terminology, an international group of experts met in Doha, Qatar. They classified groin pain in athletes into three broad categories:1

  • Defined clinical entities: adductor-related, iliopsoas-related, inguinal-related, and pubic-related groin pain.
  • Hip-related groin pain.
  • Other causes of groin pain.

Adductor-related groin pain, the category that includes classic groin strains, is the focus here.

Adductor Strength as a Risk Factor

A prospective study followed an NHL team over two seasons after preseason hip strength testing. Players who went on to sustain adductor strains had about 18% lower preseason adduction strength than uninjured players. The ratio of adductor to abductor (outer hip) strength stood out: it was about 95% in uninjured players but 78% in injured players. Players whose adduction strength was below 80% of their abduction strength were 17 times more likely to sustain an adductor strain. Notably, adductor flexibility was not different between groups.2

A later review by the same group reported that a hip-strengthening program reduced the incidence of adductor strains in one closely followed NHL team. It also highlighted prior groin injury and preseason training as risk factors.3

The Copenhagen Adduction Exercise

The strongest prevention evidence comes from soccer. The Copenhagen adduction exercise is a side-lying "plank" in which the top leg is supported by a partner or bench and the body is lifted using the inner-thigh muscles. In a cluster-randomized trial of semiprofessional Norwegian soccer teams, teams used a simple program based on this one exercise. It was performed three times a week in preseason and once a week during the season. Those teams had a 41% lower risk of reporting groin problems than control teams. Average in-season prevalence was 13.5% versus 21.3%.4

The Bottom Line

Adductor-related groin injuries are common in skating and kicking sports.1,2 Lower adductor strength, particularly relative to abductor strength, has been linked to higher risk.2 Targeted adductor strengthening, such as the Copenhagen adduction exercise, has trial evidence for reducing groin problems.4 Most of that trial evidence comes from adult male athletes, so the findings may not apply equally to every age group or sport.
