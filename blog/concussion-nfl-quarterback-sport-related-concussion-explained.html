A Quarterback's Concussion in Week 1: What Sport-Related Concussion Actually Involves

September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

In his first game with the Minnesota Vikings on September 13, quarterback Kyler Murray left in the first quarter after a hit to the side of his helmet near the end of a run. According to the Associated Press, Murray began the following week in the NFL's concussion protocol. Coach Kevin O'Connell said it was too early to know whether he would play that Sunday.1

We don't know the details of any individual player's injury beyond what has been reported. But the situation is a good opportunity to explain what a sport-related concussion is, how recovery typically unfolds, and what the research says about getting back to activity.

What Is a Concussion?

A sport-related concussion is a traumatic brain injury caused by a direct blow to the head, neck, or body that transmits force to the brain. Symptoms can include headache, dizziness, balance problems, slowed thinking, sensitivity to light or noise, and changes in mood or sleep. Standard CT or MRI scans are typically normal. Concussion is diagnosed clinically, based on the mechanism, symptoms, and examination.2

The most recent international consensus statement on concussion in sport came from a conference held in Amsterdam in 2022. It updated the standardized assessment tools used on the sideline and in the office, including the SCAT6 and the new SCOAT6.2

How Long Does Recovery Usually Take?

One of the foundational studies on this question is the NCAA Concussion Study. It followed college football players who had baseline testing before the season. After a concussion, players on average saw their symptoms resolve over about a week. Thinking (cognitive) measures returned to baseline within five to seven days, and balance deficits resolved within three to five days.3 Those are averages, and the authors emphasized that recovery varies between individuals.

Rest Isn't the Whole Answer Anymore

Concussion management used to emphasize strict rest until all symptoms were gone. The evidence has shifted. A randomized trial in adolescent athletes compared individualized aerobic exercise below the level that worsened symptoms with a stretching program, starting within the first ten days after injury. The aerobic exercise group recovered faster, with a median of 13 days compared with 17 days.4 Current consensus guidance generally supports a short period of relative rest followed by gradual return to light activity, rather than prolonged rest.2

Return to Play Is Step-by-Step

Returning to sport after a concussion follows a graduated sequence. It moves from light aerobic activity through sport-specific drills, non-contact practice, and full-contact practice, and only then to competition. Each step is completed without a return of symptoms, and medical clearance comes before full contact.2 At the professional level, that clearance process is laid out in league protocols, as the AP report notes.1

The Bottom Line

Most sport-related concussions improve within days to a few weeks.3 Early, symptom-guided activity appears to help rather than hurt.4 A structured, graduated return to play remains the standard of care.2
