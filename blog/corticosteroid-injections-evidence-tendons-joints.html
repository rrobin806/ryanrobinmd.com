Corticosteroid Injections: What the Evidence Shows About Benefits and Trade-Offs

September 25, 2026 · 6 min read

Corticosteroid ("cortisone") injections are among the most familiar treatments in musculoskeletal medicine. They are potent anti-inflammatory medications, and they can reduce pain quickly. The research is more nuanced than "they work" or "they don't." The answer depends heavily on the condition being treated and on the time frame you look at.

Tendon Pain: Short-Term Relief, Long-Term Questions

A major 2010 systematic review in The Lancet pooled randomized trials of injections for tendinopathy. For lateral elbow pain ("tennis elbow"), corticosteroid injections had a large effect on pain in the short term, around four weeks, compared with no intervention. At intermediate (about six months) and long-term (about one year) follow-up, however, the comparison flipped: patients who had no intervention were doing better. For rotator cuff tendinopathy, even the short-term benefit was unclear. Serious adverse events were rare in the trials that reported them.1

A later randomized trial in JAMA studied people with chronic tennis elbow. Compared with a placebo injection, corticosteroid injection led to worse outcomes at one year. Rates of complete recovery or much improvement were 83% versus 96%, and recurrence was 54% versus 12%.2 This research is one reason many clinicians now think carefully about steroid injections for tendon problems. (For more on why tendon pain often isn't primarily inflammatory, see our post on tendinopathy.)

Knee Osteoarthritis: A Different Picture

For knee osteoarthritis, major guidelines take a more favorable view. The 2019 American College of Rheumatology/Arthritis Foundation guideline strongly recommends intra-articular glucocorticoid injections for knee OA.3 The 2019 Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) guideline also lists them as an option for knee OA, depending on a patient's other medical conditions.4 Both guidelines position injections alongside exercise and education rather than in place of them.

Repeated injections over time are a separate question. In a two-year randomized trial in JAMA, patients with knee OA received triamcinolone (a corticosteroid) or saline injections every three months. The steroid group had significantly greater cartilage volume loss on MRI and no significant difference in pain compared with saline. The authors concluded the findings do not support that treatment approach for symptomatic knee OA.5

Why Context Matters

Taken together, the evidence suggests that corticosteroid injections can be useful for short-term symptom control in some conditions, particularly knee osteoarthritis flares. Their longer-term effects differ by tissue and condition.1–5 The decision about whether, when, and where to inject is individualized and is usually part of a broader plan that includes rehabilitation and load management. When an injection is chosen, image guidance can improve accuracy; we covered that evidence in our post on ultrasound-guided injections.
