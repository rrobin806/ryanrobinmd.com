Fall Soccer and the Warm-Up That Reduces Injuries: The Evidence Behind FIFA 11+

September 21, 2026 · 5 min read

Fall soccer is in full swing for club, high school, and recreational teams. Soccer also has some of the best injury prevention research in all of sports medicine. The intervention behind it is not a piece of equipment or a supplement. It is a structured warm-up that takes about 20 minutes.

What Is FIFA 11+?

FIFA 11+ is a standardized warm-up program that replaces a team's usual warm-up. It combines running drills with exercises for strength, balance, plyometrics (jumping and landing), and agility. The goal is to improve neuromuscular control: how well an athlete controls the body during cutting, landing, and changing direction.

The Landmark Trial

The program's best-known test was a cluster-randomized trial in Norway involving nearly 1,900 female soccer players aged 13 to 17. Clubs that used the comprehensive warm-up for a season had a significantly lower risk of injuries overall (about a 32% reduction). Overuse injuries were roughly halved, and severe injuries fell by about 45%. The trial's primary outcome, lower-extremity injury specifically, showed a similar trend but did not reach statistical significance.1

What the Pooled Evidence Shows

A 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine combined randomized trials of FIFA's injury prevention programs. When studies of the FIFA 11+ program were pooled, overall injury rates were reduced by about 39% compared with control teams. Interestingly, the older and simpler "FIFA 11" program did not show a significant effect. That suggests the content of the warm-up matters, not just the act of warming up.2

A Version for Younger Players

Younger children move and get injured differently than teenagers and adults, so researchers developed "11+ Kids" for players under 13. In a multinational cluster-randomized trial of nearly 3,900 children, teams using 11+ Kids had about 48% fewer injuries overall. Severe injuries were reduced by 74% and lower-extremity injuries by 55%. Injury rates fell further as compliance increased. The authors recommended performing it at least once a week, and ideally twice.3

The Catch: It Has to Be Done

Across these studies, one theme repeats: the benefit depends on consistent use.3 A program that is printed out but rarely run on the field is unlikely to deliver what the trials found. That is one reason these warm-ups are designed to replace a team's existing warm-up rather than add time to practice.

The Bottom Line

Structured neuromuscular warm-ups like FIFA 11+ and 11+ Kids have strong randomized trial evidence for reducing soccer injuries in youth and recreational players, and the effect appears to grow with regular use.1–3 Similar neuromuscular training principles show up in prevention research for other sports as well.
