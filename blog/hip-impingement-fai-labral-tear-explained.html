Hip Impingement (FAI) and Labral Tears: When Imaging Findings Matter

September 23, 2026 · 5 min read

Hip and groin pain in active teens and young adults often leads to imaging, and imaging often leads to two phrases: "femoroacetabular impingement" and "labral tear." Both are real and can be important. But one of the most useful things to understand about them is how often they show up in people who have no symptoms at all.

What Is Hip Impingement?

Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) describes a mismatch in the shape of the hip's ball (the femoral head) and socket (the acetabulum). In "cam" morphology, the ball is not perfectly round. In "pincer" morphology, the socket over-covers the ball. Either can cause the bones to contact abnormally at the extremes of hip motion, especially deep flexion and rotation. The labrum is a ring of cartilage that lines the socket rim, and it can be injured in the process.

Shape Alone Isn't a Diagnosis

A 2015 systematic review looked at imaging studies of people without hip pain. Cam-type morphology was common, and pincer-type morphology was reported in about two-thirds of asymptomatic hips. Among the studies that looked, labral tears appeared on MRI in about 68% of hips with no symptoms. The authors concluded that clinical decisions should weigh the history and examination carefully against the imaging.1

That is why an international group of experts introduced the term FAI syndrome in the 2016 Warwick Agreement. Under this definition, FAI syndrome requires all three of the following: hip symptoms, positive clinical signs on examination, and imaging findings. The consensus also described suitable treatments as including conservative care, rehabilitation, and arthroscopic or open surgery.2

Surgery or Physiotherapy?

The UK FASHIoN trial randomized 348 patients with FAI syndrome to hip arthroscopy or to "personalised hip therapy," an individualized, supervised physiotherapy program. Both groups improved on a hip-specific quality-of-life score at 12 months. The arthroscopy group improved more, by an average of 6.8 points on a 100-point scale. That was just above the 6.1-point threshold the investigators considered clinically important. Adverse events were somewhat more common in the surgery group, and the authors noted that longer follow-up is needed to see whether the benefit lasts.3

Put simply, both approaches helped. Surgery offered a modest additional average benefit for appropriately selected patients, along with the usual risks and recovery time of an operation.3

The Bottom Line

  • Impingement-type hip shapes and labral tears are common on imaging, even without pain.1
  • FAI syndrome is a clinical diagnosis that requires symptoms, exam findings, and imaging together.2
  • Both structured physiotherapy and arthroscopy improve outcomes, with surgery showing a modest additional benefit in one large trial.3
