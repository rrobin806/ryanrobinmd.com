Returning to Sport After Injury: How the Decision Is Actually Made

October 14, 2026 · 5 min read

"When can I play again?" is often the first question an injured athlete asks, and it is rarely a simple one. Return-to-sport decisions weigh tissue healing, function, sport demands, timing within a season, and the athlete's own goals. Sports medicine has developed frameworks to make those decisions more transparent and consistent.

Return to Sport Is a Continuum

A 2016 consensus statement from the First World Congress in Sports Physical Therapy in Bern reframed return to sport. It is not a single decision made at the end of rehabilitation; it is a continuum that runs parallel to recovery. The statement describes graded stages: return to participation, return to sport, and return to performance. It also encourages clinicians to consider biopsychosocial factors, including confidence and fear of reinjury, alongside physical measures. The authors noted that research evidence directly guiding return-to-sport decisions is still limited.1

A Framework for Weighing Risk

One widely cited framework is the Strategic Assessment of Risk and Risk Tolerance (StARRT), first proposed in 2010 and revised in 2015. It breaks the decision into three steps:2

  • Assessment of health risk: what is the state of the injured tissue, and what is the risk of reinjury or harm?
  • Assessment of activity risk: what does the sport, position, and level of play demand?
  • Risk tolerance: given factors like timing in the season, the athlete's goals, and external pressures, how much risk is acceptable?

The point of making each step explicit is to reduce conflict and confusion between the athlete, coaches, and clinicians by putting the reasoning on the table.2

What ACL Research Shows About Criteria and Timing

Some of the clearest data on return-to-sport criteria come from ACL reconstruction. In the Delaware-Oslo ACL cohort, athletes who returned to jumping, cutting, and pivoting sports such as soccer and basketball had more than a fourfold higher knee reinjury rate over two years than those who did not. The reinjury rate fell by about 51% for each month that return was delayed, up to nine months after surgery. More symmetrical quadriceps strength before return also lowered reinjury risk. Among athletes who failed the study's return-to-sport test battery, 38.2% sustained a reinjury, compared with 5.6% of those who passed.3

Those findings are specific to ACL reconstruction. Still, they illustrate a broader principle: objective measures of strength and function, combined with appropriate time for healing, tend to carry more information than time alone. (For background on the injury itself, see our earlier post on ACL tears.)

The Bottom Line

Return to sport is best viewed as a staged process rather than a single clearance date.1 Frameworks like StARRT make the risk trade-offs explicit.2 For some injuries, research supports using both time and objective testing to reduce the risk of reinjury.3
