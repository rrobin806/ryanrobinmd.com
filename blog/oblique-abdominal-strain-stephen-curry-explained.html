Stephen Curry's Oblique Tear: Understanding Oblique and Core Muscle Strains

November 23, 2026 · 4 min read

Golden State Warriors guard Stephen Curry left the team's November 11 game against the Houston Rockets early in the first quarter. He grimaced after releasing a three-pointer and headed to the locker room. According to NBC Sports, the Warriors announced after an MRI that Curry had suffered an oblique abdominal tear and would be re-evaluated in two weeks. Coach Steve Kerr said Curry described it as a tweak in his abdomen and side, something he hadn't felt before.1

We can't speak to any individual's injury beyond what has been publicly reported. But oblique strains are a well-recognized injury in sports that involve powerful twisting, so here is what the research says in general.

What Are the Obliques?

The side of the abdominal wall is made up of three layers of muscle: the external oblique, the internal oblique, and the deepest layer, the transversus abdominis. Each runs in a different direction and has a different function. Together they stretch between the lower ribs and the pelvis.2 These muscles help rotate and stabilize the trunk. They transfer force from the legs and hips through the core to the arms during throwing, swinging, and shooting.

What the Baseball Research Shows

Most of the research on oblique strains comes from baseball, where rotational force is central to pitching and hitting. A study of the Major League Baseball disabled list from 1991 to 2010 found:3

  • 393 abdominal muscle strains, about 5% of all baseball injuries, at least 92% of them involving the oblique or intercostal (rib) muscles.
  • Average time lost of about 35 days for pitchers and 27 days for position players.
  • Most injuries occurred on the side opposite the throwing arm or batting side.
  • A reinjury rate of about 12%.
  • Rates rose over the 20-year period, especially early in the season.

Possible Risk Factors

Because the core links the hips to the upper body, researchers have looked at whether hip mobility matters. A study of professional baseball players found that reduced hip internal rotation was associated with a higher risk of abdominal (core) and back muscle injuries, but not of shoulder or elbow injuries.4

Diagnosis and Recovery

Oblique strains typically cause sharp pain along the side of the trunk with twisting, reaching, or forceful movements. MRI can show which muscle layer is involved and how extensive the injury is.2 Recovery usually involves a period of relative rest followed by progressive rehabilitation and a gradual return to sport-specific movements. The baseball data show it is common to miss several weeks, and the reinjury rate highlights the importance of a complete recovery before returning.3

The Bottom Line

The oblique muscles are key to rotational power, and strains to them are a recognized injury in sports built on twisting motions.2,3 In professional baseball, these injuries typically cost several weeks and recur about 12% of the time.3 (For another common muscle injury, see our post on hamstring strains.)
