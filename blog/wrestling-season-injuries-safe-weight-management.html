Wrestling Season: Common Injuries and What Research Says About Safe Weight Management

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read

In many states, high school wrestling season gets underway in November, and with it come some injury and health issues that are fairly specific to the sport. Wrestling involves constant close contact and sudden forceful movements, and it has weight classes. Each of those shapes the risks wrestlers face. Here is what the research says about the most common problems and the rules designed to keep athletes safe.

Where Wrestling Injuries Happen

A 2026 study used national emergency department data from 2015 to 2024. It estimated roughly 370,000 wrestling-related emergency department visits in the U.S. over that decade. About 85% of these visits were for patients 18 or younger, and most were male. The shoulder (15.2%) and head (13.1%) were the most commonly injured body regions, and sprains and strains were the most common injury type. Annual visits rose from 2020 through 2024.1

Concussion is also tracked closely in the sport. A study of a large national youth wrestling tournament covered more than 58,000 wrestlers aged 13 to 18 from 2013 to 2025. It found about 0.85 concussions per 100 athletes, with a lower and declining rate in recent years.2 (For more on how concussion is assessed and managed, see our post on sport-related concussion.)

Why Weight Management Rules Exist

Weight classes create pressure to "make weight." In late 1997, three previously healthy collegiate wrestlers died within about a month of each other while trying to lose weight rapidly before weigh-ins. A CDC report described a similar pattern in all three cases. Each restricted food and fluids and tried to maximize sweating, wearing vapor-impermeable suits while exercising vigorously in hot environments, which led to dehydration and dangerous overheating.3

Scholastic wrestling has since adopted formal weight management rules. Under the National Federation of State High School Associations (NFHS) rule, which took effect for the 2004–05 season, each wrestler's minimum weight class is set in advance. This is done by a hydration test (urine specific gravity no higher than 1.025) followed by a body composition assessment, with a floor of 7% body fat for males and 12% for females. Programs also monitor weight loss using a "descent plan" that averages no more than 1.5% of body weight per week.4

The National Athletic Trainers' Association position statement on safe weight loss warns against practices like severe food restriction and fluid restriction. It recommends that weight and body composition goals be individualized and set by appropriately trained professionals.5

Skin Infections: A Wrestling-Specific Concern

Because wrestling involves prolonged skin-to-skin and skin-to-mat contact, skin infections can spread between athletes. The NATA position statement on skin diseases notes that close quarters combined with poor hygiene practices make athletes particularly vulnerable. It emphasizes prevention, early recognition, and prompt management to limit spread within a team.6

The Bottom Line

  • Shoulder and head injuries are among the most common wrestling injuries seen in emergency departments.1
  • Rapid, dehydration-based weight cutting has caused deaths, which led to today's hydration testing, body composition minimums, and gradual descent plans.3,4
  • Skin infection prevention is a team-wide responsibility in wrestling.6
