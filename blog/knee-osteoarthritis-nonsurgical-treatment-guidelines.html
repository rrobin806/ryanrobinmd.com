Knee Osteoarthritis Without Surgery: What the Guidelines Recommend

October 5, 2026 · 6 min read

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is one of the most common reasons active adults see a sports medicine physician. It is also an area where the gap between what people expect ("I'll probably need a knee replacement") and what guidelines recommend first can be large. Here is what the major guidelines and key trials say about non-surgical care.

The Core: Exercise and Education

Two major guidelines were published in 2019: one from the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI), and one from the American College of Rheumatology together with the Arthritis Foundation. Both put the same things at the center of care.

  • OARSI designates arthritis education and structured land-based exercise, with or without dietary weight management, as core treatments for knee OA.1
  • ACR/Arthritis Foundation strongly recommends exercise, weight loss for people with knee or hip OA who are overweight, self-management programs, tai chi, cane use, and tibiofemoral bracing for appropriate patients.2

"Exercise" here doesn't mean one specific program. It covers strengthening, aerobic, and neuromuscular exercise, tailored to the person.

Weight Loss Changes Knee Loads

The IDEA trial randomized more than 450 older adults with knee OA and overweight or obesity to exercise alone, diet-induced weight loss alone, or both, for 18 months. The combined diet-and-exercise group lost an average of about 11% of body weight. That group had less pain and better function than either single approach. The diet groups also had lower knee compressive forces and lower levels of an inflammatory marker than the exercise-only group.3

Medications and Injections

Topical NSAIDs are strongly recommended for knee OA by both guidelines.1,2 ACR also strongly recommends oral NSAIDs and intra-articular glucocorticoid (steroid) injections for knee OA.2 OARSI lists steroid and hyaluronic acid injections as options for knee OA depending on a person's other health conditions, and it recommends against oral and transdermal opioids.1 (We looked at the injection evidence in more detail in our post on corticosteroid injections.)

Where Does Knee Replacement Fit?

Total knee replacement is an effective operation for the right patient. A Danish randomized trial in the New England Journal of Medicine enrolled 100 people with moderate to severe knee OA who were eligible for knee replacement. They received either replacement followed by a 12-week non-surgical program, or the 12-week program alone. The replacement group had greater improvement at one year but also more serious adverse events. Importantly, most people in the non-surgical group had not gone on to replacement by 12 months.4

The Bottom Line

Across major guidelines, exercise, education, and weight management for those who need it are the foundation of knee OA care.1,2 Medications and injections can help manage symptoms.1,2 Surgery remains an option when appropriate, and a structured non-surgical program is a meaningful treatment in its own right.3,4
