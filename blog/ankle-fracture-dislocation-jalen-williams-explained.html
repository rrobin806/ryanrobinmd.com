A Fractured Fibula and Dislocated Ankle: Understanding Ankle Fracture-Dislocations

October 26, 2026 · 5 min read

Late in the fourth quarter of Oklahoma City's game against the LA Clippers on Sunday, October 25, Thunder forward Jalen Williams was injured on a drive to the rim and had to be stretchered off the court. On Monday, ESPN's Shams Charania reported that Williams sustained a dislocated left ankle and a fractured fibula and will undergo surgery. Thunder general manager Sam Presti reportedly said a more exact timeline would come after surgery.1,2

We can't speak to any individual's injury or care beyond what has been publicly reported. But an ankle fracture combined with a dislocation is a significant injury, and it's worth explaining what that combination means in general terms.

The Anatomy

The ankle joint is formed where the two lower-leg bones, the tibia (shin bone) and the fibula (the thinner outer bone), meet the talus, a bone at the top of the foot. The ends of the tibia and fibula form a socket, often called the ankle mortise, that holds the talus. Ligaments connect all of these bones and keep the joint stable.

What a Fracture-Dislocation Means

A fracture is a break in a bone. A dislocation means the bones of the joint have been forced out of their normal alignment. When both happen together, the joint has typically lost its normal bony and ligament support. These injuries usually involve high forces, such as an awkward landing with the body's weight coming down on a twisted foot.

In general, a dislocated joint is put back into place (reduced) promptly to protect the surrounding skin, blood vessels, and nerves. Unstable fractures in active people are then commonly treated with surgery to restore and hold the alignment of the joint.

Surgery vs. Casting: What Trials Show

Not every unstable ankle fracture requires surgery. A large randomized trial in JAMA studied adults aged 60 and older with unstable ankle fractures. It compared surgical fixation with a carefully applied "close contact" cast. Function at six months was equivalent between groups. Infection and wound problems were more common with surgery, while malunion (healing out of alignment) on X-ray was more common with casting.3 That trial involved older adults rather than athletes, but it illustrates the trade-offs involved in treatment decisions.

How Long Does Return to Sport Take?

Recovery timelines vary widely. A systematic review of return to sport after ankle fractures found that the general approach for acute fractures in athletes was surgical fixation. However, the authors concluded that the optimal timing of return to sport after an acute ankle fracture has not been established, because the available studies were retrospective and measured outcomes inconsistently. They also emphasized assessing and addressing associated injuries, such as ligament damage.4

The Bottom Line

An ankle fracture-dislocation means both a broken bone and a joint knocked out of alignment, and it is one of the more serious ankle injuries in sport.1,2 Treatment generally focuses on restoring joint alignment and stability, and recovery is measured in months. The specific timeline depends on the exact injury pattern and any associated damage.3,4
