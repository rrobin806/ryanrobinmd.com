Osgood-Schlatter and Sever's: Growth-Plate Pain in Active Kids

October 2, 2026 · 5 min read

Growing athletes have a vulnerability adults don't: growth cartilage. Where large tendons attach to bone, children have growth centers called apophyses. Repetitive pulling from strong muscles can irritate them. The result is apophysitis, and two of the most common forms are Osgood-Schlatter disease at the knee and Sever's disease at the heel.

Osgood-Schlatter Disease

Osgood-Schlatter disease involves the tibial tubercle, the bump just below the kneecap where the patellar tendon attaches. Repeated contraction of the quadriceps through the patellar tendon is the most accepted explanation for how it develops. It typically causes pain and tenderness over that bump, often worse with jumping, running, kneeling, or stairs. The bump may become more prominent over time.1

Sever's Disease

Sever's disease (calcaneal apophysitis) is the heel equivalent. It involves the growth center at the back of the heel bone where the Achilles tendon attaches. It is common in running and jumping sports. A 2023 systematic review of factors associated with Sever's disease found that limited ankle dorsiflexion (upward ankle bend) was the most frequently studied factor. Foot alignment, plantar pressures, body mass index, age, sex, and sport participation also came up. The authors noted that studies disagreed about which of these are true risk factors and which are simply associated findings.2

Is It Really "Self-Limiting"?

Apophysitis has traditionally been described as self-limiting, meaning it resolves once the growth plate closes. Reviews still describe the overall prognosis of Osgood-Schlatter as good.1 Newer research suggests the picture is more mixed than "it always goes away quickly," though.

A prospective study followed 51 adolescents aged 10 to 14 with Osgood-Schlatter for two years. More than a third still had knee pain at two-year follow-up, and those with ongoing pain reported lower sports function and quality of life. Participants with more advanced changes on ultrasound at the start were more likely to still have pain later.3

There is encouraging news, too. A 2024 study of adolescents in a self-management program of activity modification, education, and exercise found that almost all of those with Osgood-Schlatter reported improvement by one year. That included 89% of those who had not improved after the first four weeks. The authors highlighted the importance of sticking with the rehabilitation plan even when early progress is slow.4

The Bottom Line

Osgood-Schlatter and Sever's are common, overuse-related growth-plate conditions in active kids.1,2 Most young athletes improve, but recovery is not always quick, and some have symptoms for a year or more.3,4 For persistent pain, an evaluation can help rule out other causes and guide a structured plan that manages load rather than relying on complete rest alone.
