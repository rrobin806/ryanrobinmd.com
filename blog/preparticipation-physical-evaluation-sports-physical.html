What a Sports Physical Actually Covers (and What It Doesn't)

September 16, 2026 · 5 min read

For many families, the sports physical is a form to get signed before the season starts. The preparticipation physical evaluation (PPE) is meant to be more than that. It is a structured screening visit designed to identify conditions that could make sport unsafe and to address issues that affect an athlete's health more broadly.

Who Sets the Standard

The current recommendations come from the fifth edition of the PPE monograph. It was developed jointly by six organizations: the American Academy of Family Physicians, the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American College of Sports Medicine, the American Medical Society for Sports Medicine, the American Orthopaedic Society for Sports Medicine, and the American Osteopathic Academy of Sports Medicine.1,2 The AAP also hosts the standardized PPE forms.2

Where and When It Happens

The updated recommendations favor doing the PPE as part of an athlete's regular care with their own physician. Mass "station-based" physicals and retail or urgent-care visits are discouraged because they lose continuity and medical history. The recommendations suggest completing the evaluation at least six weeks before practices begin, so there is time to follow up on any findings. For high school and younger athletes, a comprehensive PPE every two to three years is suggested, with annual history updates in between.1

The History Matters Most

Much of the PPE's value comes from the history form. Key areas include:

  • Cardiac history and family history, aimed at identifying risk factors for sudden cardiac death.
  • Prior injuries and concussions.
  • Mental health screening, using a brief four-question tool (the PHQ-4) now built into the history form.
  • Disordered eating and, for female athletes, menstrual history.

The physical exam includes a focused cardiovascular exam (listening for murmurs, checking femoral pulses, seated blood pressure, and looking for signs of Marfan syndrome) and a musculoskeletal exam focused on past injuries and current symptoms.1

What About an ECG?

Whether every athlete should have an electrocardiogram is a long-running debate. The current PPE recommendations do not call for universal ECG screening. They note that it may be considered in higher-risk groups. When ECGs are used, the recommendations call for athlete-specific interpretation criteria.1 Those international criteria were developed so that normal heart adaptations to training are not mistaken for disease, and so that truly abnormal findings get proper follow-up.3

What a PPE Can't Do

A PPE is a screening tool, not a guarantee. It cannot detect every condition, and a normal evaluation does not mean an athlete is risk-free. That is one reason the recommendations emphasize continuity of care and an accurate, honest history over a quick check-the-box visit.1 Elite sport organizations take a similar view of periodic health evaluation as an ongoing process rather than a single test.4
