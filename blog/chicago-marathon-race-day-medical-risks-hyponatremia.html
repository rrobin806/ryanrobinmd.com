Marathon Season in Chicago: What the Research Says About Race-Day Medical Risks

September 30, 2026 · 5 min read

The 48th Bank of America Chicago Marathon takes place on Sunday, October 11.1 Tens of thousands of runners from the city, the suburbs, and around the world are in their final taper. Most race-day problems are blisters, cramps, and sore legs. But two less common medical issues get a lot of attention in sports medicine research: low blood sodium from overdrinking, and cardiac arrest. Here is what the evidence actually shows about each.

Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia: When Drinking Too Much Is the Problem

Exercise-associated hyponatremia means an abnormally low sodium level in the blood during or after prolonged exercise. In its severe form, it can cause confusion, seizures, and life-threatening brain swelling.

A landmark study in the New England Journal of Medicine tested runners at the 2002 Boston Marathon. At the finish line, 13% had hyponatremia and 0.6% had critical hyponatremia. The strongest risk factors were substantial weight gain during the race, a finishing time over four hours, and very low or very high body mass index. Notably, the type of fluid consumed (sports drink versus water) was not associated with risk.2 Weight gain during a race is a signal that a runner took in more fluid than they lost.

An international consensus statement on exercise-associated hyponatremia identifies overdrinking, meaning drinking beyond what is lost through sweat and urine, as the primary cause. It generally supports drinking according to thirst rather than following a fixed, aggressive hydration schedule.3

Cardiac Arrest: Rare, but Worth Understanding

Few things in endurance sport get more attention than race-day cardiac arrest. A study of U.S. marathons and half-marathons from 2000 to 2010, covering nearly 11 million runners, found an overall incidence of about 0.54 cardiac arrests per 100,000 participants. Risk was higher in full marathons than in half-marathons, and higher in men than in women. The most common underlying causes were hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and coronary artery disease.4

One finding stands out for race organizers and spectators: among cases with complete data, the strongest predictors of survival were bystander-initiated CPR and an underlying cause other than hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.4 This is a big part of why large marathons invest heavily in on-course medical teams, CPR-trained volunteers, and automated external defibrillators.

The Bottom Line

  • Hyponatremia is mainly a problem of drinking more than you lose, and slower finishing times and weight gain during the race are linked to higher risk.2,3
  • Race-day cardiac arrest is rare, and prompt bystander CPR is strongly associated with survival.4

Good luck to everyone toeing the line in Grant Park on the 11th.
