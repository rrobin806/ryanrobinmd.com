Tennis Elbow (Lateral Epicondylitis): What the Evidence Says

November 27, 2026 · 4 min read

"Tennis elbow" is one of the most familiar names in sports medicine, and one of the most misleading. Most people who develop it don't play tennis. The medical term is lateral epicondylitis, also called lateral epicondylalgia or lateral elbow tendinopathy. It causes pain on the outside of the elbow, often with gripping, lifting, or turning a doorknob.

What Is It?

The muscles that extend the wrist and fingers attach by a shared tendon to a bony bump on the outside of the elbow called the lateral epicondyle. Tennis elbow is a painful overload condition of that tendon. Despite the "-itis" ending, it is generally understood as a tendinopathy rather than a simple inflammation. (We explain that distinction in our post on tendinopathy vs. tendinitis.)

Who Gets It?

A population study of 4,783 adults aged 30 to 64 in Finland found that about 1.3% had definite lateral epicondylitis at the time of examination. Rates were similar in men and women and highest between ages 45 and 54.1 The study found that:1

  • Repetitive and forceful arm activities combined were strongly associated with the condition.
  • Current and former smoking were also associated with a higher risk.

That fits with what clinicians see: the condition is common in people whose work or hobbies involve repeated gripping and forceful use of the arm, whether that's a racquet, a hammer, a mouse, or a garden tool.

The Surprising Evidence on Treatment

Two well-known randomized trials changed how many clinicians think about tennis elbow treatment.

A trial in The Lancet randomized 185 patients to corticosteroid injections, physiotherapy, or a "wait-and-see" approach.2

  • At 6 weeks, injections looked best: a 92% success rate, compared with 47% for physiotherapy and 32% for wait-and-see.
  • But recurrence after injection was high, and by 52 weeks success rates were 69% for injections, 91% for physiotherapy, and 83% for wait-and-see.

A later placebo-controlled trial in JAMA enrolled 165 patients with symptoms lasting more than 6 weeks.3

  • At 1 year, corticosteroid injection led to lower rates of complete recovery or much improvement than a placebo injection (83% vs. 96%).
  • Recurrence was much higher after corticosteroid injection (54% vs. 12%).
  • Physiotherapy did not significantly change 1-year outcomes, though people who received physiotherapy with a placebo injection did better at 4 weeks than those who did not.

Taken together, these trials suggest that a steroid injection can bring quick short-term relief but may come at the cost of worse outcomes and more relapses down the road. They also show that many cases improve over time. (For a broader look at steroid injections, see our post on corticosteroid injections.)

The Bottom Line

Tennis elbow is common in middle-aged adults and is linked to repetitive, forceful arm use and to smoking.1 Many people improve with time and a gradual, active approach.2 Corticosteroid injections can relieve pain in the short term, but the evidence suggests they may worsen long-term outcomes and increase recurrence.2,3 Pain that persists, is severe, or comes with numbness, weakness, or swelling deserves evaluation to confirm the diagnosis and talk through options.
