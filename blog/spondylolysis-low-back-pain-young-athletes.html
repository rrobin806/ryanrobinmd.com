Low Back Pain in Young Athletes: Why Spondylolysis Belongs on the Radar

September 14, 2026 · 5 min read

When an adult has low back pain, the usual causes are muscle strain or disc problems, and most cases settle on their own. When a teenage athlete has persistent low back pain, the list of likely causes is different. One diagnosis in particular, spondylolysis, comes up far more often than many families expect.

What Is Spondylolysis?

Spondylolysis is a stress injury to the pars interarticularis, a small bridge of bone at the back of a vertebra, most often in the lower lumbar spine. Like other bone stress injuries, it is thought to develop from repetitive loading. In the spine, that loading is typically repeated extension (arching backward) and rotation. Those movements are common in gymnastics, football linemen, dance, diving, volleyball, and many other sports.

Why Young Athletes Are Different

A classic study from Boston Children's Hospital compared 100 adolescent athletes with low back pain to 100 adults with acute low back pain. In the adolescents, 47% were ultimately diagnosed with spondylolysis, compared with just 5% of the adults. Disc-related pain and muscle strain, the most common adult diagnoses, were much less common in the young athletes.1 The authors concluded that clinicians need to approach back pain in young athletes differently to avoid missed diagnoses and delays in treatment.

Back pain itself is common in this age group. A 2022 systematic review in the British Journal of Sports Medicine estimated that about 42% of adolescent athletes report low back pain over a 12-month period. Spondylolysis was the most frequently reported structural finding. Potential risk factors included higher sport volume and intensity, older adolescent age, and female sex.2

How It Typically Presents

Spondylolysis commonly causes low back pain that worsens with arching backward or twisting and improves with rest. Because pain from a pars stress injury can look like a simple "back strain," persistent or activity-limiting back pain in a young athlete is a common reason for a sports medicine evaluation. Imaging decisions depend on the clinical picture.

What the Research Says About Management

Most athletes with spondylolysis are managed without surgery. A review of outcomes in athletes found that conservative care, typically some combination of activity modification, physical therapy, and in many cases bracing, allowed return to sport in a weighted average of about 3.7 months. Surgical repair, generally reserved for cases that do not respond to conservative care, was associated with a longer average return of about 7.9 months. The authors noted there is no single gold-standard protocol. They also noted the studies were too varied to pool in a formal meta-analysis.3

The Bottom Line

Low back pain in adolescent athletes is common. It also has a different set of likely causes than adult back pain, and pars stress injuries are near the top of that list.1,2 The good news from the literature is that most athletes with spondylolysis return to sport without surgery, though recovery is typically measured in months rather than weeks.3
