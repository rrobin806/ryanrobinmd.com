Turkey Trot Season: Injury Risk for New and Returning Runners

November 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Thanksgiving morning is one of the biggest running days of the year. Turkey Trots draw families, first-time racers, and people lacing up for the first time in months, from neighborhood 5Ks in Bloomingdale and Schaumburg to large races across the country. That makes it a good moment to look at what the research says about injury risk for new and returning runners, and what actually seems to help.

New Runners Get Hurt More Often

A systematic review and meta-analysis in Sports Medicine pooled prospective studies to compare injury rates across different types of runners. Novice runners had a weighted injury incidence of about 17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours of running, compared with about 7.7 per 1,000 hours in recreational runners.1 In other words, hour for hour, people who are new to running appear to face roughly twice the injury risk of those who run regularly.

Where Running Injuries Happen

A systematic review in the Journal of Sport and Health Science found that the knee, ankle, and lower leg accounted for the largest share of new running injuries. The most common specific diagnoses in prospective studies included:2

  • Achilles tendinopathy (about 10% of injuries)
  • Medial tibial stress syndrome, or shin splints (about 9%)
  • Patellofemoral pain, or runner's knee (about 6%)
  • Plantar fasciitis (about 6%)
  • Ankle sprains (about 6%)

Most of these are overuse injuries, which means they develop when the load on a tissue outpaces its ability to adapt.

How Fast Should You Build Up?

The familiar "10% rule" says not to increase weekly mileage by more than 10%. The evidence behind it is more mixed than many people assume.

In a randomized trial of 532 novice runners preparing for a 4-mile race, a graded 13-week program based on the 10% rule did not reduce injuries compared with a standard 8-week program. About 1 in 5 runners in each group (roughly 20%) sustained a running-related injury.3

A prospective study that tracked 874 novice runners with GPS watches found no overall difference in injury rates across groups based on how quickly they increased weekly distance. However, runners who increased their distance by more than 30% over a two-week period appeared more vulnerable to certain "distance-related" injuries, such as patellofemoral pain, iliotibial band syndrome, and shin splints. The authors described the study as exploratory, but suggested novice runners may be well advised to keep increases below 30%.4

The practical takeaway is that no single percentage is magic, but large, sudden jumps in running volume are worth avoiding.

Practical Points for Race Morning

  • Run the pace your training supports. If you haven't been running, walking part or all of the course is a perfectly good way to take part.
  • Don't let one race become a sudden spike. A 5K is short, but for someone who hasn't run in months it can still represent a big jump in load.
  • Treat the week after as a ramp, not a restart. If the race inspires a new routine, build gradually rather than jumping straight to daily runs.
  • Pay attention to pain that persists. Pain that lingers, worsens with each run, or changes how you walk or run deserves evaluation.

The Bottom Line

New runners are injured more often than experienced ones, and most running injuries affect the knee, lower leg, ankle, and foot.1,2 Strict adherence to the 10% rule hasn't been shown to prevent injuries, but avoiding large, sudden increases in running distance appears sensible.3,4 (For more on two of the most common running injuries, see our posts on runner's knee and bone stress injuries.) Happy Thanksgiving, and enjoy the race.
