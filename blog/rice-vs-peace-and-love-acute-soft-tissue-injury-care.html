From RICE to PEACE & LOVE: What the Evidence Says About Treating Sprains and Strains

November 20, 2026 · 5 min read

For decades, the standard advice for a sprain or strain was RICE: rest, ice, compression, and elevation. Many people still reach for an ice pack and the couch as soon as they roll an ankle. But sports medicine thinking has shifted. The emphasis has moved toward early, guided movement, with more nuance about ice and anti-inflammatory medications.

What the Evidence Says About Ice

A systematic review in the American Journal of Sports Medicine examined 22 randomized trials of cryotherapy (ice) for acute soft-tissue injuries. The overall quality of the trials was low. The review found only marginal evidence that ice combined with exercise was effective after ankle sprains and surgery, little evidence that adding ice to compression made a significant difference, and no evidence of an optimal method or duration of icing. The authors concluded that many more high-quality trials were needed.1 In other words, ice may help with short-term pain for some people, but the evidence that it speeds healing is thin.

Early Movement Beats Strict Rest

A randomized trial in the BMJ enrolled 101 people with grade 1 or 2 ankle sprains. One group received standard protection, rest, ice, compression, and elevation. The other received an accelerated program that added early therapeutic exercise during the first week. The exercise group had significantly better ankle function at one and two weeks and was more active during the first week, without any increase in pain, swelling, or reinjury.2 Systematic reviews of ankle sprain treatment have similarly found strong evidence for early mobilization and moderate evidence for exercise therapy.3

The Anti-Inflammatory Question

Anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs) are a more nuanced topic. An overview of systematic reviews found strong evidence that NSAIDs reduce pain and swelling after acute ankle sprains.3 At the same time, an evidence-based clinical guideline on ankle sprains cautions that NSAIDs are not without complications and may suppress the natural healing process.4 That tension is why many clinicians now think carefully about when, and for how long, these medications are used after an injury.

From RICE to "PEACE & LOVE"

In 2019, an editorial in the British Journal of Sports Medicine proposed a new acronym that reflects this shift.5

  • PEACE (in the first days): Protect, Elevate, Avoid anti-inflammatories, Compress, and Educate.
  • LOVE (afterward): Load, Optimism, Vascularisation (pain-free cardiovascular activity), and Exercise.

Notably, PEACE & LOVE leaves out ice. It adds education, psychological factors such as optimism, and gradual loading of the injured tissue. The authors frame this as a move away from treating only the immediate injury and toward supporting the whole course of recovery.5 It is a framework rather than a clinical trial. Its individual elements draw on the kind of evidence described above.

The Bottom Line

The evidence for ice as a healing tool is limited.1 Early, appropriately guided movement and exercise generally outperform strict rest for common sprains.2,3 NSAIDs can relieve pain and swelling, but the guidance is that they be used thoughtfully.3,4 Of course, not every injury is a simple sprain. Inability to bear weight, significant deformity, or rapidly worsening swelling or pain warrant prompt evaluation. (For more on ankle sprain prevention, see our post on ankle sprains in basketball.)
