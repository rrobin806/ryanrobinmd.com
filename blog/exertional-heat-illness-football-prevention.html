Heat Illness in Early-Season Football: What the Research Says About Prevention

September 11, 2026 · 5 min read

Football season is underway, and early-season practices and games often still fall on hot, humid days, including across the Chicago suburbs. That is when exertional heat illness is most likely. Heat cramps and heat exhaustion are the common forms. Exertional heat stroke is less common, but it is a true medical emergency.

The National Athletic Trainers' Association calls exertional heat stroke one of the leading causes of sudden death during sport.1 The research also shows that much of this risk can be reduced with preparation and policy. Here is what the evidence says.

Preseason Is the Highest-Risk Window

A study of youth, high school, and college football programs tracked heat illness events recorded by athletic trainers over the 2012–2014 seasons. At every level, rates were highest during the preseason. The most common events were heat cramps, heat exhaustion, and dehydration.2 The authors noted that the overall risk was low. They also noted that the preseason pattern points to how much the first weeks of practice matter, since athletes are not yet used to working hard in the heat.

Heat Acclimatization Policies Appear to Work

In 2009, an NATA-led task force released guidelines for phasing in preseason practice gradually. The guidelines limit early practice length, equipment, and the number of two-a-day sessions while athletes adapt to the heat. Researchers later compared preseason heat illness rates in high school football between states that mandated these guidelines and states that did not. Rates were significantly lower in state-years with a mandate.3

On-Site Preparedness Is Uneven

A separate national survey asked athletic trainers about six heat stroke preparedness strategies in high school football. These included an emergency action plan, a cold-water immersion tub ready before practice, environmental (wet-bulb globe temperature) monitoring, and access to hydration. Only about 27.5% of respondents said their school used all six. The figure was higher in hotter regions with state mandates and lower in milder regions without them.4 Northern states are often assumed to be lower risk, yet that is where preparedness was least consistent.

Why Rapid Cooling Is the Priority

The American College of Sports Medicine's consensus statement on exertional heat illness describes heat stroke management as a "chain of survival." The steps are rapid recognition, stopping activity, and rapid total-body cooling, ideally on site. As the statement puts it, "time is tissue": outcomes appear to relate to how long body temperature stays dangerously high.5 The NATA position statement likewise describes cold-water immersion as the preferred cooling method and stresses having it planned in advance.1

The Bottom Line

The research supports a few general conclusions:

  • Heat illness risk peaks early in the season, when athletes are least acclimatized.2
  • Gradual acclimatization policies are associated with fewer heat illnesses in high school football.3
  • Preparedness varies widely between schools, especially in milder climates.4
  • Exertional heat stroke is an emergency where rapid on-site cooling is the central step.1,5
