Shoulder Impingement and Rotator Cuff Pain: What the Evidence Says

October 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Shoulder pain that flares when you reach overhead, sleep on that side, or lift something away from your body is one of the most common reasons people see a sports medicine physician. It goes by several names: "shoulder impingement," "rotator cuff tendinopathy," or, more recently, subacromial pain syndrome. The research on how it is best managed has changed considerably over the last decade.

What It Is

The rotator cuff is a group of four muscles and tendons that stabilize and move the shoulder. Subacromial pain syndrome describes pain arising from the area around the rotator cuff tendons and the bursa beneath the acromion, the bony tip at the top of the shoulder. The older term "impingement" suggested the tendons were being pinched by bone. That mechanical explanation has been increasingly questioned, partly because of the surgical trials described below.

Rotator Cuff Changes Are Common With Age

A systematic review pooled imaging data from more than 6,000 shoulders. Rotator cuff abnormalities increased steadily with age, from about 10% in people 20 and younger to 62% in people 80 and older. The pattern was similar in people with and without symptoms. The authors concluded that rotator cuff degeneration can be considered a common part of normal aging, which makes it hard to know whether an imaging finding is actually the cause of someone's pain.1

What Placebo-Controlled Surgical Trials Showed

For years, a common operation for persistent shoulder pain was arthroscopic subacromial decompression: shaving bone and soft tissue to "make more room" for the tendons. Two large trials tested this against placebo (diagnostic arthroscopy without the decompression step):

  • CSAW (UK): 313 patients who still had pain after exercise therapy and at least one steroid injection were randomized to decompression, arthroscopy only, or no treatment. At six months, decompression was no better than arthroscopy only. Both surgical groups did slightly better than no treatment, but the difference was not clinically important.2
  • FIMPACT (Finland): 210 patients were randomized to decompression, diagnostic arthroscopy, or exercise therapy. At two years, decompression provided no benefit over diagnostic arthroscopy.3

Based on these trials, a 2019 BMJ clinical practice guideline panel of patients, clinicians, and methodologists made a strong recommendation against subacromial decompression surgery for subacromial pain syndrome. The panel also noted that there is still substantial uncertainty about which alternative treatment is best.4

What Non-Surgical Evidence Shows

A 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine examined conservative treatments for shoulder impingement. Its findings:5

  • Exercise reduced pain compared with non-exercise controls, and specific shoulder exercises outperformed generic ones.
  • Corticosteroid injections were superior to no treatment, and ultrasound-guided injections outperformed non-guided ones.
  • NSAIDs and manual therapy showed small to moderate benefits compared with placebo.

The authors cautioned that much of the evidence was low quality. Even so, they concluded that exercise should be considered for people with these symptoms.5

The Bottom Line

Rotator cuff changes on imaging are common with age and don't always explain pain.1 Placebo-controlled trials have shown that a once-routine "decompression" surgery offers no meaningful benefit over placebo for subacromial pain.2–4 Structured, specific exercise is the treatment with the most consistent support, and injections and other therapies play a supporting role for some people.5 (These findings apply to subacromial pain, not to acute traumatic rotator cuff tears, which raise different questions.)
