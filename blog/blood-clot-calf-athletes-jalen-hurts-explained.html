A Quarterback's Calf Blood Clot: What Athletes Should Know About Leg Clots

October 16, 2026 · 6 min read

On October 15, the Philadelphia Eagles ruled quarterback Jalen Hurts out of that Sunday's game against the Carolina Panthers because of a blood clot in his calf. According to the team's account reported by ABC's Philadelphia station, Hurts had been "nicked" in the October 4 game against the Rams. He first sought treatment for his calf on October 8. A precautionary ultrasound on October 10, after the team's flight to London, showed a superficial blood clot. After review, team doctors concluded he could safely play that weekend and recommended a follow-up ultrasound. That follow-up, on the night of October 14, showed "movement in the small blood clot," and he was ruled out while the team determined the appropriate treatment.1

Update (October 19): NFL.com has since reported that Hurts' diagnosis was updated to deep vein thrombosis with clots in both lungs (pulmonary emboli). It also reported that he is being treated with blood thinners, is expected to miss the rest of the season, and that the team expects a full recovery.2

We can't speak to any individual's medical care beyond what has been reported. But leg clots are not common in young, fit athletes, so it is worth explaining the general concepts.

Superficial vs. Deep Vein Clots

Veins in the legs fall into two systems. Superficial veins run just under the skin. A clot there, called superficial vein thrombosis (SVT), often causes a tender, firm, reddened cord. Deep veins run within and between the muscles, and a clot there is a deep vein thrombosis (DVT). The main concern with DVT is that part of the clot can break off and travel to the lungs, causing a pulmonary embolism (PE), which can be serious.

Why a "Superficial" Clot Still Gets Close Attention

Superficial clots were once viewed as relatively harmless. Research has changed that view. In the POST study, a large prospective study of 844 patients with symptoms of superficial vein thrombosis, every patient had a full ultrasound of both the superficial and deep veins. About 23.5% had a deep vein thrombosis at the same time, and in many of those cases the DVT was not directly connected to the superficial clot. The authors concluded that a complete ultrasound of the leg's venous system provides information with important consequences for management.3 (Most POST participants were older than typical athletes, so the exact numbers may not translate directly to young athletes.)

Risk Factors in Athletes

Classic risk factors for venous clots include injury to the leg, immobility, dehydration, and some medications and inherited clotting tendencies. Travel is also a well-studied risk factor in the general population. In a large cohort study, the rate of venous thrombosis roughly tripled in the weeks after long-haul flights. Risk was highest in the first two weeks and returned to baseline by about eight weeks.4 The absolute risk per flight was still low.

Why Blood Thinners and Contact Sports Don't Mix

The standard treatment for DVT and PE is anticoagulation, or blood-thinning medication. While an athlete is on blood thinners, a hard hit carries a higher risk of serious internal bleeding. That is why, as reported, a return to contact football is tied to coming off anticoagulation.2

The Bottom Line

Leg clots are uncommon in athletes but important to recognize. Calf pain, swelling, warmth, or a tender cord in the leg, especially after an injury or travel, can warrant evaluation. Shortness of breath or chest pain needs urgent attention. Even "superficial" clots get careful imaging and follow-up because of their potential link to deep vein clots.3
