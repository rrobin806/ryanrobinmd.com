Bone Stress Injuries in Runners: More Than 'Just a Stress Fracture'

October 12, 2026 · 5 min read

With the Chicago Marathon behind us and fall racing season winding down, it is a good time to talk about one of the injuries distance runners worry about most: the bone stress injury. Many people know it as a "stress fracture." But that term describes only one point on a wider spectrum, and the location of the injury matters a great deal.

A Continuum, Not a Single Injury

Bone is constantly remodeling: repetitive loading creates tiny amounts of microdamage, and the body removes and replaces it. A bone stress injury (BSI) develops when microdamage builds up faster than the bone can repair it. Researchers describe BSI as a continuum that starts with a stress reaction. It can progress to a stress fracture and, ultimately, to a complete fracture.1 Bone stress injuries tend to recur in runners. The authors of one major review argue that this is why the underlying causes deserve as much attention as the injury itself.1

Two Sides of the Equation

Risk depends on two things: the load applied to a bone, and the bone's ability to tolerate that load.1

  • Load factors include training volume and how quickly it increases, as well as running mechanics and impact forces. They also include the strength and endurance of the muscles that help absorb load, such as the calf muscles for the shin bone.1
  • Bone-side factors include bone density and structure, along with hormonal and nutritional factors that affect bone health.2

Why Location Matters

Not all bone stress injuries behave the same way. A 2022 review in Nature Reviews Disease Primers describes management as guided by location and the associated risk of healing complications. Injuries at low-risk sites typically heal with activity modification followed by progressive loading and a return to activity. Additional approaches, such as non-weight-bearing immobilization, medications, or surgery, are usually reserved for high-risk sites, where complications are more likely.2 That distinction is one reason a suspected bone stress injury is usually worth a proper evaluation rather than a guess.

The Energy Availability Connection

Bone health is closely tied to whether an athlete takes in enough energy to support both training and normal body functions. The International Olympic Committee's 2023 consensus statement on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) describes how problematic low energy availability can affect many body systems, including bone health. It applies to athletes of any sex. The statement introduced an updated clinical assessment tool to help clinicians identify and stratify REDs risk.3 For runners with bone stress injuries, especially repeated ones, energy availability is an important part of the conversation.

The Bottom Line

Bone stress injuries sit on a spectrum from stress reaction to fracture.1 Their management depends heavily on where they occur.2 Prevention involves both training load and the bone's capacity to handle it, including adequate energy intake.1–3 (For shin pain that is not a bone stress injury, see our earlier post on medial tibial stress syndrome.)
